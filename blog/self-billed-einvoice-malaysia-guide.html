    Self-Billed E-Invoices in Malaysia: The LHDN Guideline Explained (2026)

    September 17, 2026 | 13 min read

    Most Malaysian businesses approached e-invoicing with one question: how do we send e-invoices to our customers? That's the visible half of the mandate, and it's where the effort went—integrating with MyInvois, validating fields, handling rejections.

    The half that gets missed is the mirror image: transactions where the buyer, not the seller, has to issue the e-invoice. LHDN calls these self-billed e-invoices. They cover situations far more ordinary than the name suggests, such as foreign software subscriptions, agent commissions and renting from a landlord who isn't in business.

    Why this matters: for these transactions, the validated self-billed e-invoice is your proof of expense. Without it, the expense may not be substantiated for tax purposes—which turns a documentation gap into a deduction you can't claim.

    Sources: LHDN's e-Invoice Specific Guideline v4.9 (sections 3.6.5 and 8) and the e-Invoice FAQ (4 September 2026). This is a plain-language summary, not tax advice; confirm your own position with your tax agent.

    What a Self-Billed E-Invoice Is

    Normally the seller issues the e-invoice and the buyer receives it. With a self-billed e-invoice, you, as the buyer, take the role of the supplier: you create the e-invoice, submit it to LHDN through MyInvois, and use the validated document as your proof of expense. The other party then doesn't need to issue one for that transaction.

    Once it is validated, you must share it with the supplier. LHDN currently accepts either the validated e-invoice or its visual representation (the version with the QR code).

    The 9 Transactions in LHDN's Guideline

    Section 8.3 of LHDN's e-Invoice Specific Guideline lists every case where the buyer issues a self-billed e-invoice. The list is closed: LHDN's FAQ says that if a transaction isn't on it, you are not allowed to self-bill it.

    1. Payments to agents, dealers and distributors. Commissions and other incentives, in money or otherwise, whether the agent is an individual or a company. The business making the payment issues it.
    2. Goods or services from foreign suppliers. Imported goods, overseas services, and the software subscriptions, cloud hosting and advertising almost every business now pays for—including purchases made through foreign e-commerce platforms. The Malaysian buyer issues it.
    3. Profit distributions, such as dividends. The company making the distribution issues it.
    4. E-commerce transactions. The platform or intermediary issues it to the merchants and service providers it pays, such as drivers and riders.
    5. Pay-outs to betting and gaming winners. The licensed operator issues it. Pay-outs in casinos and from gaming machines are exempt until further notice.
    6. Transactions with individuals who are not conducting a business. Buying a used laptop from an employee, or renting premises from a landlord who isn't in business. This applies only when none of the other cases do.
    7. Interest payments. The payer issues it, except where a financial institution charges interest to the public, an employee pays interest to the employer, a foreign payor pays a Malaysian taxpayer, a Malaysian group treasury company charges its related companies, or a business charges late payment interest. In those cases the lender issues an ordinary e-invoice.
    8. Insurance claims, compensation and benefit payments. The insurer issues it to the policyholder or beneficiary.
    9. Capital reductions, share or unit redemptions, share buybacks, returns of capital and liquidation proceeds. The company paying the investor issues it.

    What is not on the list: a Malaysian supplier who simply hasn't sent you an e-invoice. LHDN's own example is a business buying cupcakes from a roadside stall that gives a handwritten receipt: the business may not self-bill, and keeps the receipt as its evidence instead.

    A quick way to find your exposure: list every recurring payment leaving your business that does not arrive with a Malaysian e-invoice, then check each one against the nine cases above. Foreign SaaS subscriptions, cloud hosting, overseas advertising, agent commissions and rent paid to individuals usually surface immediately.

    When You Must Issue It

    Most e-invoices have no fixed deadline beyond your normal invoicing practice. Self-billed e-invoices for imports and consolidated self-billed e-invoices do:

    • Imported goods: by the end of the second month after the month customs clearance is obtained. Include the customs form reference number (for example, Customs Form No. 1).
    • Imported services: by the end of the month after the month you paid or received the foreign supplier's invoice, whichever came first.
    • Consolidated self-billed e-invoices: within 7 calendar days after the month end.
    • Capital reductions, redemptions, buybacks and similar: on the date of the written agreement, or of government approval where that is required, or on completion where there is no written agreement.
    • Everything else: in line with your normal practice. Agent commissions can follow the periodic statements you already send, and both agent payments and interest can be done on an accrual or a paid basis, as long as you apply it consistently.

    When one foreign invoice is shipped in several parts, and the invoice was issued by the time the first shipment cleared customs, you issue one self-billed e-invoice for the first clearance and none for the later shipments.

    Which TIN to Use for the Supplier

    The supplier on a self-billed e-invoice is often someone with no Malaysian tax number. LHDN provides general TINs for these cases:

    • Foreign supplier with no Malaysian TIN: EI00000000030. Check with the supplier first: the general TIN is only for suppliers LHDN hasn't given a TIN.
    • Individual who gives only a MyKad, MyTentera, MyPR or MyKAS number: EI00000000010, with that number as their ID.
    • Individual who gives only a TIN: that TIN, with 000000000000 as the ID number.
    • Consolidated self-billed e-invoice: supplier name "General Public", TIN EI00000000010 and registration number NA.

    When You Can Consolidate

    By default, every self-billed transaction gets its own e-invoice. Even invoices from the same foreign supplier can't be combined. Consolidation is allowed only for:

    • Transactions with individuals who are not conducting a business
    • Interest paid to the public at large, whether businesses or individuals
    • Insurance claims, compensation and benefits paid to individuals not in business, or to government bodies
    • Self-billed transactions involving your own overseas branches or offices

    Imports: Three Details People Get Wrong

    • The value. Use the actual transaction value on the foreign supplier's invoice. Extra charges under the agreed incoterms go in the "other charges" details.
    • Duties and taxes. Leave out the import duty and sales tax collected by Customs. They don't belong on the self-billed e-invoice.
    • Currency. The e-invoice can be in the supplier's currency. Add the RM equivalent only where a tax rule requires it.

    What Your System Needs to Support

    Here's where a lot of legacy systems fall short. Most accounting and ERP systems built before the mandate have a single invoicing path: sales out, purchases in as recorded documents. Self-billing needs something different.

    1. Creating an outbound document for an inbound transaction

    Your system has to generate an e-invoice where your own business is the issuer and a third party—often foreign, often without a Malaysian TIN—appears as the supplier. MyInvois has its own document types for this: 11 for a self-billed invoice, and 12, 13 and 14 for self-billed credit, debit and refund notes. Many systems simply have no screen for it.

    2. Handling supplier identification edge cases

    Foreign suppliers won't have a TIN in the usual format, and individuals may not have a business registration number. The submission still has to pass validation using the general TINs above. Hard-coded local-format validation is a frequent breakage point.

    3. Submitting, sharing and storing the validated document

    The self-billed e-invoice goes through MyInvois validation like any other. You need the validation response, the unique identifier and the QR-bearing document stored against the expense record, and a way to send the validated copy to the supplier.

    4. Keeping it out of your sales figures

    A self-billed e-invoice is an expense document that happens to be issued by you. If it leaks into revenue reporting because it was built on top of your sales invoice module, your accounts will disagree with your tax position.

    The Practical Approach for a Legacy System

    You don't necessarily need a new system. In most of the legacy .NET and SQL Server setups we work with, self-billing is handled by adding a parallel document path rather than rebuilding invoicing.

    1. Identify which of your payment flows trigger self-billing. Check them against the nine cases. Usually it is a short list: foreign subscriptions, overseas services, commissions, rent to individuals.
    2. Decide where the document originates. Often the purchase or payment entry you already capture—so the self-billed e-invoice is generated from data staff are entering anyway, not typed a second time.
    3. Add a document type rather than a new module. The same submission plumbing that sends your sales e-invoices can send self-billed ones, with the party fields reversed and the type set to 11.
    4. Accept LHDN's general TINs for non-Malaysian and individual suppliers. EI00000000030 for foreign suppliers and EI00000000010 for individuals, instead of your existing local-format checks.
    5. Track the deadlines. Imported goods and services have month-end deadlines that run from customs clearance or payment, so the system should know which date applies.
    6. Store the validated result against the expense. So that when a query comes, the substantiation is one click from the transaction rather than a manual search.
    7. Run a back-check on the period since the mandate applied to you. Find the transactions that should have had self-billed documents and work out, with your tax agent, how to address them.

    Common Mistakes We See

    • Assuming foreign purchases are out of scope. The supplier being outside Malaysia is exactly what creates the obligation, not what removes it.
    • Self-billing things that aren't on the list. A Malaysian supplier who hasn't sent an e-invoice is not a reason to self-bill. Only the nine cases qualify.
    • Consolidating foreign purchases. A month of invoices from the same overseas supplier still needs one self-billed e-invoice each.
    • Adding import duty and sales tax. The value is what the foreign supplier charged. Customs duties and taxes stay off.
    • Recording them as sales. Built on the sales module without a separate document type, they end up inflating revenue.
    • Doing it manually in the portal and never reconciling. Portal-entered documents that don't flow back into your system leave your records and your submissions permanently out of sync.
    • Leaving it to year-end. Import deadlines run month by month. Reconstructing months of documents at once is far more expensive than issuing them as you go.

    The Bottom Line

    Self-billed e-invoices catch businesses out precisely because they don't look like invoicing. They come out of your payments, not your sales, and they involve suppliers who are entirely unaware of Malaysian e-invoicing rules.

    The rules themselves are narrow: nine cases, a few deadlines and four general TINs. Most systems need a second document path, not a replacement—but it does need to be deliberate, because nobody is going to chase you for an invoice you were supposed to write to yourself.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Do I need a self-billed e-invoice for overseas software subscriptions? +

    Yes. Services from foreign suppliers are one of the nine self-billed cases, and software and cloud subscriptions are the ones most businesses overlook because the amounts are small and recurring. Issue it by the end of the month after the month you paid or received the invoice, whichever came first.

    Can I issue a self-billed e-invoice if my Malaysian supplier didn't send me an e-invoice? +

    No. Self-billing is only allowed for the nine transactions in Section 8.3 of LHDN's e-Invoice Specific Guideline. If a supplier who should e-invoice you hasn't, ask them for one; if they aren't yet required to, keep their receipt or invoice as your evidence.

    What TIN do I use for a foreign supplier with no Malaysian TIN? +

    EI00000000030, LHDN's general TIN for foreign suppliers on self-billed e-invoices. Check with the supplier first, because the general TIN is meant only for suppliers that LHDN hasn't given a TIN.

    Can I combine all my invoices from one foreign supplier into one self-billed e-invoice? +

    No. Each transaction with a foreign supplier needs its own self-billed e-invoice. Consolidation is allowed only for individuals not in business, interest paid to the public, certain insurance payments and transactions involving your own overseas branches.

    Can I just issue self-billed e-invoices through the MyInvois portal manually? +

    For low volumes, yes. The risk is reconciliation—documents created in the portal that never make it back into your accounting records leave your books and your submissions disagreeing, which is painful to unwind later.

    Does this apply if I'm not yet in an enforced phase of the mandate? +

    Your self-billing obligations follow the same phase timing as the rest of your e-invoicing obligations. If you're already issuing e-invoices to customers, assume self-billing applies to you now.

    How much work is it to add self-billing to an existing legacy system? +

    Usually less than owners fear, if the system already submits e-invoices. The submission plumbing is reused; the work is in a new document type, accepting the general TINs and storing the validated result against the expense. It's typically a scoped project, not a rebuild.

    What happens if we've missed self-billed e-invoices for past months? +

    Address it deliberately rather than quietly. Identify the affected transactions, discuss remediation with your tax agent, and close the system gap so it stops accumulating—an ongoing gap is a much bigger problem than a historical one you're fixing.
