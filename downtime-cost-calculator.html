What this leaves out

The direct cost is the part you can count. The rest rarely shows up in the accounts: the regular customer who goes elsewhere, orders delivered late, records re-entered by hand, and the hours a manager spends calming things down. Outages also tend to grow. A system that needs a restart once a quarter today can need one every week in two years, if the cause is left alone. What happens when you delay maintenance walks through how that unfolds.

Common questions

How do I calculate the cost of downtime?
Add three things for each incident: the sales you lose while the system is down, the pay of staff who can't work, and any recovery cost such as overtime or an emergency fix. Multiply by the number of incidents a year. The calculator shows the formula with your numbers.
What doesn't this calculator count?
Customers who don't come back, damage to your reputation, late deliveries, data that has to be re-entered or corrected, and the management time spent dealing with it. These are often larger than the direct cost, but they are too specific to each business to estimate here.
Are all sales lost while the system is down?
Not always. Some customers wait or come back later. Lower the share of sales lost to reflect that. A shop with walk-in customers usually loses more than a supplier whose customers will reorder tomorrow.
I don't know how often we go down. What should I enter?
Count the times in the last year that staff couldn't use the system, including slow restarts and the morning nobody could log in. If nobody tracks this, start keeping a simple log; it is the first number anyone fixing the system will ask for.
How do I reduce downtime?
Fix the recurring problems behind it rather than restarting around them, test that backups actually restore, and know how long a recovery takes. Addressing warning signs on a schedule is cheaper than fixing a failure under pressure.
